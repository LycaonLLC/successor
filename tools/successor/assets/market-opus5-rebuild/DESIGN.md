# Design language — Valley Market (scheme E)

Fresh art direction for the settlement market. No inherited reference image or
brief was used. The only outside context read was the allowed terminal
manifests, which establish a palette of basalt, ceramic, brass, steel, teal
glass and amber/cyan glow. The building is authored to *host* those fixtures
without copying their material system. The settlement is referred to
generically here: the place name those manifests use was rejected at review,
and no replacement has been chosen.

This document describes the **built** scheme. The first authored pass
("Sinter-Frame Civic", a stepped box) was rejected at the human review gate;
`ALTERNATIVES.md` records the five schemes compared afterwards and why E won.

## 1. Parti: water is the thing worth expressing

On an inhabited desert planet, a market is where a settlement's two scarce
things change hands — goods and water. So the roof is not a lid, it is a
catchment, and the building's primary gesture is the path water takes:

**butterfly valley → east sump → downpipe → cistern drum → rear plant**,
with an overflow that stains the north wall in exactly one place.

Every roof decision follows from that: the fold, the gutter, the sumps, the
drum on its cradle, the crickets that divert water around each penetration.

## 2. Tectonic premise — two ways of building, one visible seam

- **Below 2.42 m — sintered basalt mass.** Solar-sintered from local sand into
  thick monolithic walls. Heavy, warm dark, made *on site*, so it is imprecise.
  It is thermal mass, sand-blast armour and ballast. It takes every impact:
  kerbs, bollards, benches, ledges, pier bases.
  **At every outside corner this mass returns as an expressed pier** — proud of
  the wall field, its outer arris cut back at 45°, standing on a splayed base
  course with a weathered top, with a brass impact guard across the chamfer at
  the height trolleys and shoulders actually strike, and a brass capping under
  the datum band. The corner is where the structure turns, so the corner is
  where the mass thickens; the panel field spans between.
- **2.42–2.56 m — a brass datum band.** Not decoration: it is the drip flashing
  and the tolerance joint between something cast in place and something
  delivered on a pallet. It is the only element that runs continuously around
  the whole building, and it is what the water streaks fall from.
- **Above 2.56 m — imported ceramic-composite panel.** Thin, pale, precise,
  set back 0.07 m so the seam reads as a shadow line. Expensive, so it is used
  only where it must be: the upper walls and the fold.

Steel appears where something is engineered (track, rafters, clerestory posts,
brise-soleil, plant), brass where something is touched or sheds water (datum,
reveals, handles, treads, thresholds, copings). Plaster appears only on interior
soffits. Nothing is trimmed in brass "for looks" — this was the pass-1 failure.

## 3. Massing hierarchy

Four shapes at four heights, none of them repeated:

| element | height | section |
|---|---|---|
| windcatcher tower | 6.60 m | square, louvred head facing north |
| cistern drum | 6.18 m | cylinder on a steel cradle |
| flue stack | 5.79 m | round, coned and cowled |
| entry hood | 4.60 m | **the only curve on the building** |

The butterfly roof folds about a valley at y +1.85 which sits **directly over
the interior service wall**, so the fold has a load path. South plane falls
16.7 % from a 4.42 m eave to the 3.56 m valley; north plane is lifted **0.84 m**
to 4.40 m at the valley and rises 7.9 % to a 4.58 m eave. That lift is the
clerestory, and it is deliberately held just below the hood apex (4.58 < 4.60)
so the entrance is still the tallest event on the public face.

## 4. The south face is stepped three times

Because a market's public side should be a sequence, not an elevation:

1. **west loggia** (pier line y −3.98) — three *unequal* bays (0.46 / 0.38 /
   0.32 m piers), a coffered soffit, a goods ledge, a bench, a shopfront. It is
   deliberately partial: it stops before the entry, so it never becomes a ring
   of identical columns (the trap scheme D fell into).
2. **entry hood** (face y −4.16) — one vault, 3.80 m wide, 0.48 m deep ring,
   apex 4.60 m. It **breaks the south eave**, which is what makes it read as the
   arrival from both eye level and the pitched game camera. Its keystone carries
   the building's one focal insert: a machined brass boss, two turned rings
   stepping down into a recess, a lamp ring and a glass roundel, and in front of
   them a small brass mark — a hub and four unequal spokes, goods arriving from
   four ways. No lettering and no settlement name.
3. **trainer bay** (face y −3.72) — a lower volume (2.94 m) tucked under the
   main eave, with its own shaded window and roof.

## 5. Repetition is allowed only where it does work

The brief rejects repetition that reads as a stamp. The one repeated element on
the building is the **brise-soleil over the clerestory** — three blades and four
unequal arms. It now oversails 0.63 m *above the head* of the glazing rather
than hanging across it, for a reason that is pure geometry and is recorded in
§6a. Everything else that could have become a pattern was deliberately made
unequal: rafter bays (1.88 / 4.60 / 2.16 m), loggia bays, collector panel
widths, vent positions, tower sections, service-wall piers, flank fittings.

Roof seams survive only inside the service-walk bay and the two verge bays, at
0.026 × 0.028 m. They are construction evidence, not a texture.

## 6. Interior: one tall room, one low room

- **Public hall** — from the entry to the service wall, under a raked plaster
  soffit falling 4.17 → 3.40 m. Pendants on the circulation spine, a queue rail
  set south of every promised approach cell, a vendor display line on the west
  wall with shelves, hanging rack, bins and scale.
- **Service wall** — 0.34 m thick. It rises to **2.86 m** between four unequal
  expressed piers which continue to 3.38 m and carry the fold. Above the wall
  head a **glazed transom** fills the bays to the hall ceiling line, so the hall
  reads a lit band over the counter and the back of house keeps its separation.
  Three deep niches with brass reveals, backing panels, transaction slots,
  shutter boxes and downlights, so a terminal is a *counter in a wall*, not a
  hole punched to daylight. Geometric identifiers only — bars, cross, ring, set
  on each dock head's own fascia where the queue actually sees them. No
  lettering.
- **Back of house** — a real staff aisle behind the niches, top-lit by the
  clerestory, with dropped soffits over the two plant bays, shelving, a bench,
  switchgear, a water tank, and a ladder to the roof hatch.
- **Trainer bay** — south-east, its own low ceiling, a consultation table at the
  bay mouth with the trainer behind it and the visitor drawn up to its north
  side, and a **skills assessment bay**: a calibration column the customer
  stands at, a lit readout with a graduated brass scale that reports the
  reading, and a rack of demonstration instruments in slotted keeps. The
  storage is a waist-height credenza in the bay's dead corner, so nothing
  blocks the seat, the approach or the sightline.

## 6a. The daylight section, and the trade it makes

The north plane's lift is a south-facing clerestory over the staff aisle. Making
it *legible* forced three changes and exposed one piece of geometry that cannot
be had both ways:

1. **The service wall head came down** from 3.35 m to 2.86 m between its piers.
   At 3.35 it stood directly in front of glazing starting at 3.60, so no
   standing person could see the clerestory at all.
2. **The valley beam moved south** of the glazing plane, under the south deck
   edge, where it is also the expressed brass-faced edge of the hall ceiling.
   North of the glass it cut every standing sightline to the sill.
3. **The brise-soleil moved above the head.** A louvre passes rays shallower
   than `atan(spacing/depth)` and blocks steeper ones. To block a 42° sun the
   cut-off must be under 42° — and a standing eye 1.35 m from the band looks
   **up at 56°**. So a brise that genuinely shades this glass also blocks every
   interior view of it. The two cannot both be had at this section. The blades
   therefore step south and up from the head and oversail 0.63 m: they shade the
   band above z 3.894 at a 42° sun (about half the aperture at midday, more
   morning and evening), they cast the shadow that gives the fold depth in the
   pitched game view, and they leave the aperture clear from inside. The
   remaining gain is carried by the tinted glazing. This is a trade, not a win.

Measured from the shipped geometry: from standing eye height (1.65 m) in the
open middle of the staff aisle, 35–55 % of sampled rays land on the glazing, and
**every one of them continues outside the envelope** — it is an aperture, not a
panel. The back of house measures 3.25× brighter with sun and sky than with the
authored emissive fixtures alone.

## 6b. The two flanks do different work

The side elevations answer to the rooms behind them, and they are not mirrored.

- **West — goods and structure.** A sintered buttress at the valley line, the
  one place on this flank where a real load lands; a shuttered goods hatch so
  stock reaches the vendor end of the hall without crossing the customer
  entrance; and the west valley sump's hopper, downpipe, shoe and spill block —
  which is what makes the water stain authored on that wall true.
- **East — water and plant.** A brass draw-off standpipe with valve wheel, hose
  coupling and a trough, because water is the settlement's other trade and this
  is where containers get filled; an isolator cabinet on a raised bund, louvred,
  outside the switchgear it serves; and the conduit drop that links the roof
  plant to it.

## 7. Wear responds to use, not to a noise function

Tiles carry micro-detail only. Everything at architectural scale is authored per
vertex: contact grime at **24** named places where hands, goods and feet actually
go — now including the goods-hatch ledge, the standpipe wheel, the trough splash
and the trainer's calibration plate; water streaks only beneath the datum band,
the cistern overflow and the west sump downpipe; wind-blown dust on up-facing
surfaces low down; short-ray contact AO in creases. A surface is dirty because
something happens there.

**And most surfaces are not dirty at all.** Base colour is flat unless the real
material genuinely has different minerals at the surface. Only two do: sintered
basalt (fused aggregate) and ground screed (cut aggregate faces exposed by
grinding). The other five — ceramic, plaster, brass, steel, galvanised sheet —
are manufactured surfaces whose character is *specular*, so their float texture,
crazing, turning marks, brush grain and spangle live in the normal and roughness
maps where they are physically causal. Every material carries more micro detail
in roughness than in albedo. The palette separates by value (0.057–0.569 linear,
a 10× spread), roughness (0.23–0.89), metallic response, aggregate scale
(0.30–2.40 m tiles), edge treatment and authored wear — not by seven versions of
the same speckle.

## 8. Where the sun is

The building's public face and its clerestory both face **south**, so the
renders put the sun in the **south-east at 42° altitude**. This is stated here
because for three passes the render rig's sun sat in the north-west, which left
the entire public facade in shade in every proof and meant the south-facing
clerestory never received a single direct ray — the brise-soleil was shading
nothing. Diagnostic crops use a 20° grazing sun raked *from the side of the
corner being examined*, because a corner lit from the opposite side is the least
informative picture available.
